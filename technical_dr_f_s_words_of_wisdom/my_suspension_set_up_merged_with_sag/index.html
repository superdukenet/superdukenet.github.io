Hi guys,

In the last few weeks I've been trying to set up the suspension on my SuperDuke R 2008. I've made very good progress but I'm not there yet. I'm posting this thread because I've been speaking to the-bunker quire a bit about it and I'm hoping more people will chime in. I've also noticed that spartaro87 has posted a .

Either way. My set up is for British back roads. These roads are sometimes quite awful with loads of roots, changing surfaces, patches, horse pats, etc. IMO, the stock set up is too hard, both compression and damping.

Check that your sag is correct before you start this all. There's plenty of resources out there on how to do it. Here's one from . I'm 90kg with all my gadgets, leathers and helmet on, so I'm probably slightly above average. Fact is I've not bothered setting this as it feels OK and the zip tie at the front fork leg kind of confirms it.
Setting the sag first is because you want the suspension to use all available movement.

The next step is to set the suspension back to standard. You do this by reading the manual. The SDR requires a flat screwdriver and a 17mm spanner. All available in the tool kit.
Then, I recommend that you print my , print it double sided. Then you get a short pencil, and a screwdriver that won't puncture your lungs if you have an off. Grind a coin if you need to improvise.
Keep this with you at all times.

The PDF contains one column for every setting (on the SDR). On one side you have the default settings and some information. On the other side there's the settings. First thing to do with the settings side is to jot down the starting points (which you get from the manual or the sheet).

This is how you get to your suspension setting.
You ride your bike (d'Oh!!!) and then you stop and make an adjustment, and it's absolutely vital that you write down what setting you've changed. Also write down a comment (typically on the previous line) about what you thought the bike felt like.
Every setting has got a "Change" and a "Result" column. In the "Change" column you set + or - and the number of clicks (or turns for the Hi Speed Compression). Then you sum it up into the "Result". For example, I started out with Front Compression at 18 (in the "Result" column), I then decreased the damping, which means I screwed the screw out 2 clicks. In my notes I thus entered +2 in the "Change" and in the "Result" column 20.
Try to adjust as little as possible every time you're adjusting something. The more you fiddle with, the more difficult it is to "feel the change". I.e you can't easily put down which turn of which knob made the difference. Constrain yourself. When you've made your knobturns and you've made a note of the change. Go ride. Preferably ride the same roads you rode before. Ideally create a 10mile (16km) loop or so, that you can ride after every change. When you've formed an opinion of the suspension, stop, write it down and do another change. Keep doing this until you kick the bucket. What I mean by that is that your bike will be different, the roads will change, the climate will change, your riding will improve, and so forth. You can always tweak the suspension a bit. Typically it will take you about a month of casual riding to get to "your base" setting.

A bit of my theory on suspension.
Suspension is too hard for the UK roads. Mainly it's the compression damping that's too stiff. When you hit a bump, such as a manhole cover, and the bump gets pushed up through the bars then the front is too much damped on the compression. If the same thing happens to your arse and you feel like you're about to fly off the seat, then the rear compression damping is too hard.
I.e hit a bump, and if you feel it in the bike (arms and arse) too much, it's too hard. The too much part is vital as you still want some feel of the bumps.
Then another sensation you can look out for; the Pogo-Stick sensation. If you go into a dip, or land after a slight crest, and you feel the bike wants to send you off like you'd be bouncing on a pogo stick, then your rebound damping is too low. This feeling is the result of having the bikes weight compress the springs, and then sending that same power that pushed the springs down, back out.

I'm now going to try to reduce the bike bouncing upwards when it hits the bumps, and focus my main damping on the rebound side. There's a few issues with this rather one-sided damping. First of all, any compression will happen quite easily. This happens when accelerating (easy wheelies) and braking (bad braking), but also it might reduce the ground clearance in corners quite radically.
The other effect of little compression damping is that if you have too much rebound damping, then the bike will never be able to extend back after one or more consecutive bumps. The syndrome is called "packing", because the suspension packs down.
So, the ideal is to have reduced the compression damping so much that bumps don't feel harsh, but the bike floats over them, but still retaining enough compression damping to feel the road, and not impair braking and cornering. Then use the rebound damping to make sure that I'm not sent skywards when the suspension extends, and more importantly that rebound damping is not so hard that the bike's prone to packing.

This might not suit you at all, and I doubt it'll be good on a flat race track. British back lanes, on the other hand, I think the concept is a winner.

Currently my settings are:
Front Compression: 25 clicks
Front Rebound: 16 clicks
Rear Low Speed Compression: 26 clicks
Rear High Speed Compression: 3.25 turns (fully out)
Rear Rebound: 10 clicks

I'm still working in the direction to soften the compression damping, but last adjustment I felt something went out of balance. I might have to go back one setting and take it from there. Either way, this is where the notes come in handy! And also, don't be afraid to reset your tinkering to standard. There's 3 (or rather 4) settings than you can try out from the manual.

The more you tinker (don't forget the notes notes!!) the more you'll learn about your suspension.

Finally I want to say that I'm no expert, and that these settings should be considered guidelines only. Don't stick the settings on your bike and get on the bike and ride it as a dork. The bike's characteristics will have changed and might catch you out resulting in an accident. That's bad. One step at a time.

Have fun!

SAG -

Rider sag is the distance from full extension (wheel off floor) to the amount the suspension drops (sags) with the rider on the bike (full riding gear on)

Static sag is the distance from full extension to the amount the suspension drops under the weight of the bike only.

rider sag aim for 30-35mm rear and 35-45mm front.
static sag aim for 5-10mm rear and 25-35mm front.

Just as examples my statics are 9-10mm rear, 29-30mm front. Rider sags are 30mm rear and 45mm front.

This web site here is as good as any to fully expalin setting sags -


http://www.moto-racing.co.uk/Guides/mot ... nSetup.htm

With the rear shock just spray some WD40 on the top of the adjuster and it will soak into the threads. Leave it to soak for a while then undo the collar locking screw, grab the collar and spring by hand and you should be able to turn it. Failing this use a peice of wood and a hammer to turn it.

NOPE

This is when you find out if the springs work for your weight.

for example, you set the rear static sag at 5mm but find the rider sag is 50mm, youve now run out of adjustment as you cant go lower than 5mm static and this would tell you your rear spring was far too soft for your body weight.

If your of average weight you should be able to come up with a comprimise where by your rider sag and static sag are within acceptable ranges!

If youve measured rider sag then youve done the hard part, measuring static sag is a single handed 2 minute job

On a seperate note i would be tempted to knock a couple of mm off the front sag (this would be 2 full turns anti clockwise on the pre load adjusters) this will improve turn in on the brakes by lowering the front end slightly

OK, the sport settings are too hard for the road, (infact the rear shock sports settings are too hard for anything including track). Make sure you read the manuel and your turning the adjusters the correct direction!!!!!!!!

Try these -
Road settings -
Fork
rebound - 8 clicks from full hard
Comp - 14 clicks from full hard

Shock
High speed compression 1.5 turns anti clockwise (1.5 turns for everything, this is 1 adjustment youl never need to touch!!!!!)
Comp 15 - 17 clicks from full hard
Rebound 15 - 17 clicks from full hard

Track settings -
Fork
rebound - 6 clicks from full hard
Comp - 10 clicks from full hard

Shock
High speed compression 1.5 turns anti clockwise (1.5 turns for everything, this is 1 adjustment youl never need to touch!!!!!)
Comp 12 clicks from full hard
Rebound 11 clicks from full hard

The above should give you a good base setting for road and track. Set it for road and give it a try, just remember its going to feel stiffer now anyway as you have set your rear sag correctly. KTM set them up real soft at the rear as standard

if you are used to a hard setup in the front (giving lots of feel), you may not be used to how the sd's suspension works best... as the sd has much more travel and a dirt bike style needle in the fork valves.... not the long fine ones used in most sport bike suspension like showa or ohlins. it works better when it feels softer.

it may not feel right at first (especially coming from a sportbike) but you will find that the suspension can now work properly and maintain grip with the road even over b and c grade surfaces
